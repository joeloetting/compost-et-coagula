Those are actual notes from Old Hundred, with the words *We'll crowd thy gates with thankful songs*. You can close the words, open the source, or replay their appearance. Most of the time, while reading an essay, you would simply pass over them.

I am learning about this music as I go. What follows explains how the site turns a passage from a book into that little interruption. You do not need to read music or write code to follow it. There are a few details for anyone who wants to look under the hood, too.

## Getting the notes onto the page

Each phrase has a record: which book, which page, which voice, which verse, and the notes and words being quoted. The site reads that record and draws an SVG—a picture made from lines and shapes that stays sharp when enlarged.

No automatic scan-reading happens here. Someone has to check the transcription against the source. The program can help catch inconsistencies: if the key and pitch imply one head but the record says the printed page has another, it stops and asks for a check. It cannot tell us that we read the page correctly in the first place.

The layout has two simple rules. Higher notes go higher on the page. Longer notes get a little more horizontal space. “A little” matters: giving every long note its full proportional width would stretch a short ornament into a long strip.

For the technically curious, the current spacing rule is:

```text
space after a note = 6.5 + 4.6 × sqrt(duration in quarter notes)
```

A quarter note gets about 11.1 drawing units; a half note gets about 13.0. There is a limit of sixteen notes and rests, and the renderer tries to keep the phrase compact without squeezing the heads together. Height follows steps between written note letters, rather than exact distances in sound. The missing staff, clef, and key signature mean the ornament cannot tell you everything needed to sing it.

The thin rule running through the middle is just the section divider. It does not restore a missing staff line.

## Making the ink a little uneven

This is the part I wanted to see rather than merely describe. Below is the same phrase twice, enlarged so the surface is easier to inspect. The first has the texture filter switched off. The second has the screen's ink treatment. Both retain the small irregularities built into the outlines.

<ShapeNoteInkExample phrase="old-hundred-tenor" />

The shapes begin as drawings: triangles, ovals, squares, and Swan's less familiar forms. Their proportions come first. Then the code nudges their edges by tiny amounts, softens the corners, and varies the darkness and stem width a little. An open head has a real hole cut into it; it is not a white shape pasted over a black one.

A second layer adds fine edge movement and small gaps in the ink. This uses an SVG filter. It is meant to suggest a printed impression without turning the page into an imitation of a damaged book.

Here is Mear enlarged too. The open heads make a useful check: if the texture fills in their interiors or destroys their shapes, the effect has gone too far.

<ShapeNoteInkExample phrase="mear-tenor-s1-l1" />

The irregularity stays put. The phrase's identifier and each note's position supply a seed—a repeatable starting point for the small variations. Reloading the page does not give the same note a newly wobbly outline. With the same data and renderer settings, the SVG stays the same, although browsers can draw its pixels slightly differently.

For readers who enjoy the machinery: the outline seed uses FNV-1a and Mulberry32. The outer edge is nudged by at most 0.16 drawing units. Fill opacity varies from 0.86 to 0.98 before rounding. The filter uses one noise field for a displacement of scale 0.55 and another as a fine ink mask. Those numbers are design choices, not measurements of a historical press.

## How a phrase gets picked

There are two kinds of chance here. The ink uses repeatable variation. Picking a phrase is an actual random draw, made while writing and saved afterward.

The present method is straightforward:

1. Give the break a name in the essay.
2. Gather the catalog entries that have not already been used or reserved, leaving demonstration entries out.
3. Draw one entry, with an equal chance for each available record.
4. Save it against that essay and break.
5. Read the source and decide what to do with the encounter.

The next build does not choose again. Neither does the next reader. An unfinished draft's assignment is reserved too, so another essay cannot accidentally claim it. If the available phrases run out, the command stops; it does not quietly recycle them.

There is an optional rule to exclude the same musical passage with different words. Otherwise those are separate records. This also means the draw is equal between records, not between tune titles: a tune with three available excerpts has three chances.

The author still has plenty to answer for. I chose the collection and the break, and I decide how to respond. Chance does not make the interpretation for me.

## Some breaks can stay empty

At present, I can simply leave a break without notes. The program does not yet draw silence as a saved result.

One possible extension would first ask whether a break is eligible for music at all. Some would stay deliberately empty. For the others, a draw could choose silence or a phrase, then save either result. The chance of silence would be set separately from the number of hymns in the catalog, so adding more music would not slowly crowd it out.

That is a proposal, rather than a feature already working. It would also leave the more difficult question with me: which spaces should I avoid filling in the first place?

## A screen is not a singing

The notes appear in sequence, but their animation is not a performance. They fade in over 480 milliseconds each, staggered by 35 milliseconds; they do not follow the tune's tempo or note lengths. Reduced-motion settings leave them still.

The words and source use ordinary HTML disclosures. Keyboard and touch work without needing the animation, and without JavaScript. Screen-reader users get the title, words, source, and a sequence of the musical syllables, although that is not a complete description of the music.

On paper the notes stay, the texture filter is removed, and the hidden words and source panel do not print. An ordinary footnote is therefore needed to carry the quotation's source into print. The small irregularities in the outlines remain.

I like that the little ornament has several distances built into it: from a roomful of singers to a printed page, from the page to a catalog record, and from that record to a few marks between paragraphs. The source remains available for anyone who wants to follow them back.
