> The Church knows that all totalities of the world—society, the state—are false gods, and therefore lies. Lies have no legs to stand on. When the Church takes these lies seriously, it is lost.
>
> —Karl Barth

If we swap "Church" for "community," Barth’s warning becomes eerily relevant to our moment. The grand narratives of our age—neoliberalism, nationalism, economism—masquerade as inevitable truths. They are the false gods we are taught to take seriously. To dismantle them, we need a different way of seeing the world. The philosopher Timothy Morton offers a crucial concept: subscendence, the idea that any whole is always smaller and more fragile than the sum of its parts.

Subscendence reveals that every system, from our bodies to our politics, exists only through the messy, unpredictable interplay of its components. To embrace this fundamental fragility is to reject both fear and hope—the twin crutches of false totality. It is to move forward instead in a spirit of play, of perichoresis—a divine dance with the known and the unknown.

## I. The Subscendent Revolt: Fragility as Freedom

Subscendence dismantles the illusion of stable, unified systems. A human body is not a unified whole but a subscendent ecosystem; remove its bacterial microbiome, and the "self" collapses. This insight denies totality any ontological privilege. There is no "natural order" that justifies oppression, only shifting alliances and contingencies.

This is not merely abstract. The biosphere, often framed as a "delicate balance," is in truth an ever-shifting web of interdependencies. Stability is a comforting myth; radical fragility is closer to reality. Climate collapse is not a shocking break from an established order, but a brutal confirmation of subscendence. The task, then, is not to "save the planet"—yet another totalizing fantasy—but to learn how to live within its collapse, embracing its chaotic reality rather than denying it.

## II. Play as Post-Hope Praxis

If seriousness is the currency of false totalities, play is the praxis of subscendence. To play is to reject the demand for legacy, results, or redemption. A child builds a tower and laughs when it falls; the joy is in the making and unmaking. In this vein, degrowth need not be a narrative of scarcity, but a reorientation toward a different kind of abundance. Play shifts our energy from the impossible task of "saving the world" to the vital work of living in it.

This echoes Victor Turner’s concept of "anti-structure," where rituals like carnival temporarily subvert social hierarchies to reveal their absurdity. While the court jester may not overthrow the king, their mockery exposes the contingency of power. The Fool knows that true freedom isn't found in winning the game, but in refusing to accept its rules as absolute.

## III. Banishing Hope, Embracing the Unwritten

Hope is perhaps the last and most seductive false god. It coaxes us into passivity—waiting for a technological savior, for the revolution to arrive, for history’s arc to bend toward justice. But hope, like all totalizing narratives, is a lie. It demands faith in futures we cannot control and distracts from the work at hand. As Miguel De La Torre critiques, the narrative of "salvation history" is an illusion that asks us to accept present suffering for a future redemption that may never come.

To embrace hopelessness is not despair; it is liberation. It is freedom from the demand to conform to fraudulent narratives of progress. The time, energy, and creativity once sacrificed to empty promises can be reclaimed for what is real: mutual aid, shared meals, spontaneous acts of care. Not because they will "fix" the world, but because they are, in themselves, enough. This is an ethics para joder—not to build a utopia, but to disrupt the oppressive structures that demand our obedience now.

## IV. The Fool’s Grace: Dancing in the Ruins

In the Tarot, the Fool (<FigureRef to="fool" />) is the unnumbered card, both beginning and end. They walk to the cliff's edge not from naive optimism, but because the journey itself is freedom. For me, this Fool's journey is animated by grace.

This grace is not a personal "get-out-of-hell-free card," but the wild, unearned abundance that subverts all systems of control and scarcity. It appears foolish. It refuses to align with any empire, ideology, or power structure. An environmentalism rooted in grace is neither fear-driven nor hope-driven; it is a joyful, playful participation in our ecological entanglement. Grace terrifies systems built on control because it declares, "There is enough," dismantling the myth of scarcity. Grace is sufficient.

Grace is not safe, in fact it can be scary. Grace does not offer complacency. Grace cracks us open, revealing the messy splendor of our fragile world and freeing us to act. It liberates us from the paralysis of shame and allows us to work not for moral superiority, but for the sake of the work itself.

The climate crisis will not be "solved." The nations will not be "saved." But in the ruins of those illusions, we are finally free to dance, to tend our gardens, and to watch as the world, in its beautiful and terrifying fragility, cracks open with life.
